“Kun faya kun”
A phrase carried into filmi verse from scripture: be, and it is. Creation needs no labour — the wish alone is the act. In this song it lands less as theology than as relief: the moment you stop struggling and hand yourself over, things begin to move on their own.

Kun Faya Maula Auliya Fana
Kun verb · command form
“Be.” One syllable that starts everything. It is sung here as an exhale rather than an order — the surrender is the point, not the power.
In this song it carries the whole refrain. In ghazal lyrics the same word usually appears inside a longer phrase and reads far more plainly.

What the whole song means
It is a song about letting go of the self — the singer arrives worn out, and leaves having stopped insisting on being anyone in particular.
The refrain repeats a phrase about divine will so many times that it stops being a claim and becomes a rhythm to breathe with. The verses name a shrine and its saint, which places the song in a specific devotional practice rather than a general spirituality; the qawwali rhythm underneath does the same work. By the end the word fana — being dissolved — is offered as a relief rather than a loss.
